# Filing Status and Dependent Status — Step 1, Lines B and C
## Description
Illinois generally copies the federal filing status, with one relabel and two situations where a couple that filed jointly federally SHOULD file separately in Illinois. Line C — whether someone else can claim you — is a separate box that controls the exemption and the household-credit-style cliffs later on.
## Line B — the five statuses
| Federal | Illinois box |
|---|---|
| Single | Single |
| Married filing jointly | Married filing jointly |
| Married filing separately | Married filing separately |
| Qualifying surviving spouse | WIDOWED |
| Head of household | Head of household |
For exemption purposes single, head of household, widowed, and married filing separately are all one column ($2,925 personal exemption); only joint filers get two.
## Joint federal, separate Illinois — when and how
1. INJURED SPOUSE: your spouse owes a government debt you are not responsible for. File separately in Illinois so IDOR cannot take your share of the refund. If you file jointly, the entire refund can be taken
2. MIXED RESIDENCY: one spouse is a full-year Illinois resident and the other is a part-year resident or nonresident (military assignments are the classic case). File separately; the resident spouse files as a resident, the other files Schedule NR. Filing jointly instead treats BOTH as residents for the full year (with Schedule CR relief for the other state's tax)
In both cases: do NOT recompute anything federally. Divide each line of the joint federal return between the spouses on the Allocation Worksheet (income Lines 1-15, adjustments 16-29, AGI Line 30), and each spouse's Column B or C total becomes their Line 1. Federal community-property law governs the split for community-property income.
Timing: the election may be made until the EXTENDED due date (October 15, 2027 for tax year 2026) and is then irrevocable for that year.
## Line C — dependent status
Check "You" and/or "Spouse" if someone else CAN claim you as a dependent, whether or not they actually do. Consequences:
- Exemption allowance: lost entirely if your base income exceeds $2,925 ($5,850 joint if both boxes checked) — see ../income/exemption-allowance.md
- The IL-1040 filing threshold for a dependent is $2,925 of base income
- The Illinois EITC is unavailable to a childless filer who can be claimed by someone else
## Notes
- Widowed status carries no extra exemption; it is a federal rate-bracket benefit with no Illinois analogue
- Married filing separately still requires each spouse's name and SSN on the return
- Separate Illinois returns each need their own Schedule ICR and IL-E/EITC; the property tax credit cap and the $750 K-12 cap are shared between spouses, and each dependent goes on only one return
- The injured-spouse election is the Illinois substitute for federal Form 8379; there is no Illinois injured-spouse allocation form
## Required Information
- Federal filing status
- Each spouse's residency for the year
- Whether either spouse owes a government debt
- Whether anyone can claim you or your spouse
## Questions
- What is your federal filing status?
- Were you and your spouse both Illinois residents for the full year?
- Does your spouse owe back child support, student loans, or another government debt?
- Can a parent or anyone else claim you as a dependent?
## Common Errors
- Checking "Qualifying surviving spouse" — Illinois calls it Widowed
- Filing jointly when a spouse's debt will swallow the refund
- Filing jointly with a nonresident spouse without realizing it makes them an Illinois resident for the year
- Recomputing federal amounts on split returns instead of allocating
- Leaving Line C blank because the parent chose not to claim the dependent
## Prompt
- Should my husband and I file separately in Illinois?
- I am a qualifying surviving spouse federally, what do I check in Illinois?
- My wife owes back taxes, will Illinois take our joint refund?
