# Resident Credit (Taxes Paid to Another Jurisdiction)
## Description
Nonrefundable credit that keeps a New York resident from paying full tax twice on the same income. New York taxes residents on ALL income wherever earned, so without this credit a New Jersey commuter or a landlord with out-of-state rentals would be taxed twice on the same dollars.
## The limit that defines the credit
The credit is the LESSER of:
1. The tax ACTUALLY IMPOSED by the other jurisdiction on the doubly taxed income, or
2. The New York tax on that same income
This means moving to a HIGHER-TAX state does not produce a refund from New York. If New Jersey taxes income at a higher effective rate than New York would, the excess is simply lost — New York credits only up to what New York itself would have charged.
## What income qualifies
Income must be SOURCED to the other jurisdiction under that jurisdiction's rules AND included in New York AGI. Typically:
- Wages for work physically performed in the other state
- Business income from a trade or business carried on there
- Rental and real property income from property located there
NOT eligible: interest, dividends, and most intangible income, which is sourced to your state of residence and taxable only by New York.
## The convenience-of-the-employer trap
New York applies a CONVENIENCE OF THE EMPLOYER rule to nonresidents working for New York employers. Several states apply the same rule in reverse. A New York resident working remotely for a Connecticut or New Jersey employer can find the SAME DAY claimed by both states, with neither granting relief. Remote work arrangements across state lines need looking at directly rather than assuming the credit will clean up the result.
## Tax imposed, not tax withheld
The credit is based on tax IMPOSED — the liability computed on the other state's return — not on the amount withheld from paychecks. Over-withholding in the other state does not increase the New York credit. You must actually file the other state's return to know the number.
## Canada
Canadian PROVINCIAL income tax uses Form IT-112-C. Canadian FEDERAL tax does not qualify — that goes to the federal foreign tax credit. No other country qualifies for either New York form.
## PTET interaction
When another state's pass-through entity tax is paid on your behalf, whether New York grants a resident credit for it depends on how that state's regime is structured and has been the subject of specific New York guidance. Do not assume. Check the current TSB guidance for the particular state before claiming.
## Notes
- Separate forms are required for each jurisdiction; submit them all
- You must file the other state's return before you can complete Form IT-112-R accurately
- The credit is nonrefundable and generally does not carry forward, so it is lost if it exceeds New York tax
- New York City residents get no city-level version — the credit reduces state tax only
## Required Information
- A completed copy of the other jurisdiction's return
- Income sourced to that jurisdiction, by category
- Tax actually imposed by that jurisdiction
- Your New York AGI and New York tax
- Days worked in each location, for wage allocation
## Questions
- Which other states or jurisdictions taxed your income?
- Have you filed those nonresident returns yet?
- What tax was IMPOSED there, as opposed to withheld?
- Were you working remotely, and where was the employer located?
- Was any of the income interest or dividends, which do not qualify?
## Common Errors
- Using withholding instead of tax imposed
- Claiming a credit for tax on interest, dividends, or other intangible income
- Claiming for a foreign country other than Canada
- Using Form IT-112-R for Canada instead of IT-112-C
- Filing the New York return before the other state's return is computed
- Assuming another state's PTET automatically generates a resident credit
## Prompt
- I live in New York and work in New Jersey.
- I paid income tax to two states last year.
- I have rental income from Florida and Pennsylvania.
