# New York Source Income for Nonresidents
## Description
A nonresident pays New York tax ONLY on NEW YORK SOURCE INCOME. What counts as sourced to New York is therefore the entire question, and for remote workers New York answers it more aggressively than almost any other state.
## What is New York source income
- WAGES for work PERFORMED IN NEW YORK STATE
- Income from REAL PROPERTY located in New York
- Income from a BUSINESS, TRADE, PROFESSION, OR OCCUPATION carried on in New York
- Gain from the sale of New York real property, and from certain partnership or membership interests
- Lottery winnings over $5,000 from New York wagers, and certain gambling winnings
## What is NOT New York source income
- Interest, dividends, and most intangible investment income
- Gain on the sale of intangible personal property, unless connected to a New York business
- PENSION INCOME, which federal law protects. See below
- Wages for work performed entirely outside New York
## Pensions are protected by federal law
Under SECTION 114 OF TITLE 4 OF THE U.S. CODE, states may not tax the retirement income of former residents. Excluded categories include simplified employee pensions, certain annuity plans and contracts, certain individual retirement plans, eligible deferred compensation plans, certain governmental plans, and certain trusts.
Such pension income should NOT appear in the New York State amount column of Form IT-203.
The exception: pension income NOT described in section 114, derived from a business, trade, profession, or occupation PREVIOUSLY CARRIED ON in New York, IS New York source income. A nonresident in that position may still claim the $20,000 pension and annuity income exclusion.
## THE CONVENIENCE OF THE EMPLOYER RULE
This is the most consequential rule on this page and it surprises nearly every remote worker.
IF YOU ARE A NONRESIDENT WHOSE PRIMARY OFFICE IS IN NEW YORK STATE, YOUR DAYS TELECOMMUTING ARE CONSIDERED DAYS WORKED IN NEW YORK — unless your employer has established a BONA FIDE EMPLOYER OFFICE at your telecommuting location.
So a software engineer living in North Carolina, working from home five days a week for a Manhattan employer, generally owes New York tax on ALL of that income. The days are New York days because working from home was for the EMPLOYEE'S CONVENIENCE, not the employer's NECESSITY.
### Bona fide employer office
TSB-M-06(5)I sets out a factor test — a primary factor, secondary factors, and other factors. In general, UNLESS YOUR EMPLOYER SPECIFICALLY ACTED to establish a bona fide employer office at your location, you continue to owe New York tax on telecommuting income.
The primary factor is whether the home office contains or is near specialized facilities that cannot be made available at the employer's New York location. Meeting it is rare. Most remote arrangements fail this test.
### What this means in practice
- A remote employee should NOT assume that leaving New York ends their New York tax
- The employer's WITHHOLDING often continues, correctly
- The employee's HOME STATE generally gives a credit for the New York tax, which usually prevents double taxation but not always in full
- Two states can claim the SAME DAY when the home state applies its own sourcing rule, and neither is obliged to yield
## Nonresidents and the local taxes
| Locality | Nonresident liability |
|---|---|
| New York City | NONE. The city has no nonresident income tax |
| Yonkers | YES, the nonresident earnings tax, if you earn wages or carry on a trade or business there, or are a partner in a partnership that does |
A commuter into Manhattan owes the city nothing. A commuter into Yonkers files Form Y-203.

- TSB-M-18(2)I, nonresident partner's gain or loss on certain sales or transfers of a partnership or membership interest
- TSB-M-15(7)C, (6)I, corporate tax reform and S corporation shareholders
- TSB-M-10(9)I, income received by a nonresident related to a business previously carried on in New York
- TSB-M-09(5)I, amendment to the definition of New York source income
- The Nonresident Audit Guidelines, which set out how the Department actually examines these questions
## Required Information
- A day-by-day log of where work was physically performed
- Employer's primary office location
- Any written establishment of a bona fide employer office at your location
- New York wages from Form W-2 box 16
- New York real property income and sale proceeds
- Schedule K-1 New York source amounts
## Questions
- Where is your employer's primary office?
- How many days did you physically work in New York?
- Do you work remotely, and did your employer formally establish an office at your location?
- Do you own real property in New York?
- Do you work in Yonkers, which has a nonresident earnings tax?
## Common Errors
- Assuming remote work outside New York is not New York source income
- Allocating wages by days worked at home without meeting the bona fide employer office test
- Including pension income in the New York amount column when federal law protects it
- Assuming a nonresident owes New York City tax
- Overlooking the Yonkers nonresident earnings tax
- Failing to keep a contemporaneous work location log
