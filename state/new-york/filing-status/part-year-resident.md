# Part-Year Residents
## Description
You are a New York part-year resident if you meet the definition of resident for only PART of the year — typically because you moved into or out of the state. File FORM IT-203, not Form IT-201.
DO NOT FILE FORM IT-201 if you moved into New York on any day other than January 1, or out on any day other than December 31.
## How the tax is computed: the income percentage method
This is the mechanic that surprises people. New York does NOT simply tax the income you earned while living here at graduated rates.
1. Compute your tax AS IF YOU WERE A FULL-YEAR RESIDENT, on ALL of your income for the whole year
2. Determine an INCOME PERCENTAGE: New York source income divided by federal income
3. Apply that percentage to the full-year tax
The consequence: OUT-OF-STATE INCOME AFFECTS YOUR NEW YORK RATE even though it is not itself taxed. A person who moves to New York in November after a high-income year elsewhere pays New York tax on the November-December income at a rate driven by the whole year's income.
This is not double taxation and it survives challenge, but it makes New York look more expensive than a simple proration would.
## What is in the New York amount column
- ALL income for the period you were a RESIDENT, wherever earned
- Only NEW YORK SOURCE INCOME for the period you were a NONRESIDENT
See state/new-york/filing-status/nonresident-source-income.md for what counts as source income during the nonresident portion.
## SPECIAL ACCRUALS
The rule most often missed in a move year. You are subject to special accrual rules if you have ACCRUED INCOME and you were a nonresident on December 31 of the prior year, or will be a nonresident on January 1 of the following year.
You have accrued income if you have an item of:
- NON-NEW YORK SOURCE income that was FIXED AND DETERMINABLE in a year BEFORE the current one but is reported federally in the current year, or
- Income FIXED AND DETERMINABLE in the current year but reportable federally in a LATER year
The modifications are ADDITION CODE A-115 and SUBTRACTION CODE S-129 on Form IT-225.
What triggers it in practice: deferred compensation, installment sale gains, stock options and RSUs vesting around the move, and severance. Anyone leaving New York with unvested equity should be screened specifically.
## Full-year state residents can be part-year CITY residents
Moving from Manhattan to Westchester in June makes you a FULL-YEAR NEW YORK STATE RESIDENT and a PART-YEAR NEW YORK CITY RESIDENT. You file FORM IT-201 — not IT-203 — and attach FORM IT-360.1, Change of City Resident Status.
This is the most common part-year city situation and it does not change which main return you file. The same applies to a move into or out of Yonkers.
| Situation | Main form | Attachment |
|---|---|---|
| Moved into or out of New York STATE | IT-203 | — |
| Moved between NYC and elsewhere in New York State | IT-201 | IT-360.1 |
| Moved between Yonkers and elsewhere in New York State | IT-201 | IT-360.1 |
See state/new-york/taxes/new-york-city/nyc-part-year-resident.md.
## Credits are affected
- The EMPIRE STATE CHILD CREDIT requires FULL-YEAR residency. A part-year resident does not qualify at all
- The REAL PROPERTY TAX CREDIT and COLLEGE TUITION CREDIT likewise require full-year residency
- The EARNED INCOME CREDIT is computed as if full-year, then multiplied by the New York resident fraction
- NYC credits prorate by months of city residence from Item F
- Nonresidents and part-year residents must reduce the earned income credit allowed by the household credit allowed, and these credits may not exceed the tax liability
The loss of the Empire State child credit is the largest single consequence for a family that moves mid-year.
## Married couples with different periods
If one spouse is a full-year resident and the other a part-year resident, separate New York returns are generally required — the resident on IT-201, the part-year resident on IT-203 — unless you elect to file jointly on IT-201 as if both were full-year residents, which subjects all income to New York tax.
## Documenting the date
Residency changes when DOMICILE changes, or when the statutory residency test stops being met. Moving belongings is not by itself a change of domicile. Keep the lease, the deed, the closing statement, utility transfer dates, and records of where your family lived.
Remember that the 184-DAY STATUTORY TEST can make you a FULL-YEAR resident despite moving out, if you kept a permanent place of abode here and were present 184 days or more.
## Required Information
- Exact move date with documentation
- Income and deductions allocated between periods
- Federal income and New York source income, for the income percentage
- Accrued income items spanning the move
- Months of city or Yonkers residence, if applicable
## Questions
- On what date did you move into or out of New York?
- Did you keep a home in New York after moving out?
- How many days were you in New York after the move?
- Do you have deferred compensation, unvested equity, or an installment sale spanning the move?
- Did you move within New York State, between the city and elsewhere?
## Common Errors
- Filing Form IT-201 after a mid-year move into or out of the state
- Filing Form IT-203 after a move WITHIN New York State, when IT-201 plus IT-360.1 is correct
- Prorating income directly instead of using the income percentage method
- Missing special accruals on deferred compensation or equity
- Claiming the Empire State child credit as a part-year resident
- Assuming part-year status when the 184-day test makes you a full-year resident
## Prompt
- I moved to New York in August.
- I left New York for Texas in the middle of the year.
- I moved from Brooklyn to Long Island.
